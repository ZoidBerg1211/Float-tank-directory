About floating

What Is Floating?

The Basics

Floating means lying in a shallow pool of water saturated with Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate) — dense enough that your body rests on the surface without any effort to stay afloat. Tanks are kept dark, soundproofed, and set to skin temperature to cut down on outside sensory input.

You'll see the same thing called several different names: isolation tank, sensory deprivation tank, flotation tank, float pod, float cabin, or sensory attenuation tank. In research literature it's usually called REST — Restricted (sometimes "Reduced") Environmental Stimulation Therapy.

A Short History

Floating traces back to 1954, when neuropsychiatrist John C. Lilly built the first isolation tank while at the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health. Lilly was testing a real open question in brain science at the time: whether the brain would go quiet without outside stimulation, or stay active on its own. His original tank submerged users almost entirely, breathing through a mask.Source 1

Floating stayed mostly a research tool for close to two decades. In 1972, Glenn Perry — who had attended one of Lilly's workshops — began selling the first commercial tanks, closer to the open, salt-saturated design used today. The 1980 film Altered States, loosely inspired by Lilly's work, brought floating into public awareness, though hygiene concerns in the 1980s slowed the industry's growth for a time.

The Science

Modern float tanks use around 10 inches of water saturated with Epsom salt, dense enough (roughly 1.25–1.26 specific gravity — denser than the Dead Sea) that anyone floats face-up without effort. Water and air are kept at skin temperature, so it becomes hard to tell where your body ends and the water begins. Combined with darkness and silence, the goal is to reduce external input — touch, sound, sight, even the ordinary pull of gravity on your muscles — and let the nervous system settle.

One common claim worth a note: some float centers describe Epsom salt as something your skin absorbs for a magnesium boost. Current research doesn't support that — a 2017 peer-reviewed review found no good evidence of meaningful magnesium absorption through skin.Source 2 In a float tank, Epsom salt's actual job is simpler: it makes the water dense enough to float in.

What the Research Actually Shows

Floating has a real and growing body of research behind it — though the strength of the evidence varies by outcome.Source 3

  • Anxiety and stress

    Best-studied

    The best-studied effect. In a study of 50 people with anxiety and depression, a single hour-long float produced a large drop in state anxiety, along with reduced stress, muscle tension, and negative mood.Source 4 It was a single-session study with no placebo control, so how long the effect lasts, and how it compares to a relaxing hour spent another way, is still being worked out.

  • Blood pressure

    Single study

    A controlled study of a single floatation-REST session found it lowered both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, alongside reduced anxiety and greater calm.Source 5 That was a single session, in a sample selected for anxiety rather than blood pressure — whether the effect holds up with repeated floating, or specifically in people with diagnosed hypertension, hasn't been tested in a controlled trial yet.

  • Pain, including fibromyalgia

    Mixed evidence

    Some float centers and small, non-peer-reviewed industry studies report short-term reductions in pain, muscle tension, and stress for people with fibromyalgia after a few float sessions. Evidence here is mixed, though: a placebo- and waitlist-controlled randomized trial of floating for chronic pain found no significant difference between floating and either control group on pain outcomes.Source 6

  • Exercise recovery

    Small studies

    Some studies report improved perceived recovery and reduced soreness after intense exercise, though the studies involved are still small.

Overall, floating is well-studied enough to say it produces real, measurable relaxation effects in the short term. Claims beyond that — permanent pain relief, a cure for a specific condition — go further than the current research supports.
